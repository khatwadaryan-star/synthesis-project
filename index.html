AI and the Future of Work

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is no longer a futuristic concept, as it is now a rapidly advancing technology that is reshaping the economy and redefining the nature of work. From manufacturing to customer service, AI is becoming increasingly capable of performing tasks once reserved for humans. As such, according to an article from the Stanford Report, “AI’s introduction into all workplaces could spur one of the most consequential transitions in history” (Hoover 2026). This paper is intended for students, educators, policymakers, and other individuals seeking to better understand the social and economic impacts of AI in the workplace. This audience likely already understands the basic concept of AI and its growing presence in the labor market but may need a clearer understanding of how different groups in this issue are affected by said presence and why they disagree about its consequences. By examining the positions of five key stakeholders (employees, employers, governments, investors, and consumers), this analysis can help readers better understand the competing interests that shape the debate surrounding AI and employment. To analyze these differing perspectives, this paper uses stasis theory, a framework for examining arguments by identifying the key points at which people agree or disagree. The first stasis (fact) asks whether an issue exists. The second stasis (definition) asks how the issue should be characterized or understood. The third stasis (quality) considers whether the issue is beneficial or harmful. The fourth stasis (policy) focuses on what actions should be implemented in response to the issue. Stasis theory reveals that while each of the five key stakeholders agrees on the notion that AI driven job displacement is an existing phenomenon, they disagree on how it should be interpreted, whether it is good or bad, and what should be done about it.

Employees

Employees are the most directly affected by this issue, as they hold jobs that are at risk of being displaced by AI. A Reuters/Ipsos poll showed that "half of Americans fear that the rise of AI could put them or someone in their household out of work" (Lange & Rozen 2026). Through stasis theory, employees acknowledge that AI can replace jobs; they define AI-driven job displacement as a threat to their employment and source of income; regarding quality, they largely view widespread automation by AI negatively; and on policy, they support worker protections like retraining programs and stronger labor laws. The position employees have on this issue reflects their desire to preserve their livelihoods in the face of technological change. They would rather AI be used to assist them and improve their personal productivity in their job, as according to a Stanford HAI report, "a growing body of research confirms that AI boosts productivity and, in most cases, helps narrow skill gaps across the workforce." (Maslej 2025). Employees' beliefs in workers' rights and personal financial security make them strong advocates for policies that mitigate their displacement.

Employers

Employers have an active stake in this issue, as they hold the choice to utilize AI as a replacement for their employees. Tech.co reports that "a recent study found that half of CEOs believe they may replace jobs with AI, with the figure rising for C-suite executives" (O'Sullivan 2026). Through stasis theory, employers agree that AI is transforming the job market; they define AI-driven job displacement as a progress in technology that can improve productivity and profits; regarding quality, they largely view widespread automation by AI positively; on policy, they support laws that encourage AI innovation and allow for flexibility in AI adoption. The position employers have on this issue reflects their economic incentive behind using AI in the workplace. Along with the lower labor costs and increased productivity that comes with AI adoption, IBM notes that "the majority of business leaders believe that their competitive advantage will primarily stem from the sophistication of their AI models" (Downie & Hayes 2024). Employers' values of innovation and efficiency make them strong advocates for policies that promote worker displacement via AI.

Governments

Governments are directly related to this issue, as they are responsible for managing the economic and social effects of AI replacing jobs. According to a report by the Yale Journal of International Law, "periods of AI-induced disruptive transition are likely unavoidable, which alone justifies anticipatory policy measures" (Chen 2025). Through stasis theory, governments acknowledge that AI is reshaping the nature of work; they define AI driven job displacement as an industry wide workplace transition that requires regulation; regarding quality, they view widespread automation by AI both positively and negatively; on policy, they seek out a balance by promoting AI innovation while implementing measures to protect workers and help them adapt to the changing economy. The position governments have on this issue reflects their simultaneous desire for economic growth via technological innovations and prevention of mass unemployment. As revolutionary as AI can be for the economy, the World Economic Forum states that "without appropriate government regulations, there remains a risk that technological development will be focused on replacing human work, which could increase inequality and unemployment" (Zahidi 2025). Governments' beliefs in economic growth and public welfare make them strong advocates for a balanced set of policies when it comes to AI and its impact on labor.

Investors

Investors in AI have an active stake in this issue, as they drive AI adoption in the workplace by funding innovation and receive increased returns on their investment the more AI is adopted. ChiefAIOfficer.com states that "when the investment community expects an AI-induced labor market transformation, their capitol allocations ensure that prediction materializes" (Daigle 2025). Through stasis theory, AI investors agree the technology is changing work; they define AI driven job displacement as an innovation that can reshape the entire economy; regarding quality, they largely view widespread automation by AI positively; on policy, they support regulations and subsidies in favor of AI innovation that can maximize the profitability of their investment. The position of AI investors on this issue reflects their financial motivations behind worker displacement. The Economic Times notes that "asset managers, retail investors, and global giants are all convinced that AI is going to reshape the world" (Hussain 2025). AI investors' values of profitability and personal financial security make them strong advocates for policies that prioritize AI, even at the expense of workers.

Consumers

Consumers are unique in that they are the only stakeholder out of the five who have a passive stake in this issue, as they indirectly benefit from the replacement of jobs by AI. A recent study from ScienceDirect found that "customers generally support AI adoption when it leads to cheaper goods and services, even if they are aware of potential job losses" (Wang 2025). Through stasis theory, consumers acknowledge that AI is shifting the nature of work; they define AI driven job displacement as a convenient tool that can lower the price of goods and services; regarding quality, consumers largely view widespread automation by AI positively; on policy, consumers support further innovations in and adoptions of AI that translate into a reduction of their cost of living. The position of consumers on this issue reflects their desire for reduced costs when it comes to worker displacement. However, they do not want AI adoption that would harm their wallets, as the World Economic Forum mentions how "companies seeking to transform their business models through AI should, as long as they are creating value for consumers" (Kumar 2025). Consumers' beliefs in affordability and convenience make them strong advocates for policies that support AI innovation that will in turn ease their economic situation.

Synthesis

Stasis theory reveals both consensus and conflict amongst the five listed stakeholders in this issue. All agree on the notion that AI is reshaping the labor market. However, they diverge when it comes to the definition, quality, and policy associated with the issue. With definition, employees see displacement as a threat; governments see it as a transition, consumers see it as convenience, and employers and investors both see it as profit. With quality, employees largely view displacement negatively; governments view it with neutrality; and employers, investors, and consumers largely view it positively. Finally, with policy, employees demand protection from displacement, employers and investors push for innovation, consumers seek flexibility, and governments strive for balance. This synthesis shows that the debate is not about whether AI is changing the job market but about how those changes should be interpreted and dealt with. The described divergences stem from the different underlying values that shape each stakeholder's position on the issue. Due to this, the Economic Times states that among different stakeholders in the issue of displacement, “there is no consensus on how severe AI-driven job displacement will ultimately become” (Mallidi 2026).
